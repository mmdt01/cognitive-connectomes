\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:introduction}

% Sigma is the operating-point axis, the one that moves in a real brain through neuromodulation and arousal

The connectivity of neuronal networks is believed to lie at the heart of their processing abilities. 
Implementing brain-derived connectivity maps as untrained recurrent networks has become a standard way 
to ask which topological features support computation. However, how those features shape the geometry of 
the network's activity, and how that geometry sets computational capacity, remains unclear.

A robust finding across neural systems is that activity does not explore the available state space freely but 
is confined to a low-dimensional manifold. Between network structure and the resulting dynamics lies the 
connectivity matrix’s eigenspectrum. Its eigenvectors identify modes, coordinated patterns in which groups of 
units rise and fall together, and its eigenvalues determine how strongly each is sustained. This depends not only 
on the connectivity but on how hard the network is driven: scaling the weights up moves every mode together, 
from activity that dies away to activity that is sustained indefinitely, and networks are conventionally compared 
at a single setting of this gain. The modes are the manifold's axes, and the eigenvalues fix its extent along each; 
only those axes carrying activity that varies independently are usable. 

Here, we use this correspondence to compare a human structural connectome mode by mode against a ladder of 
null networks that preserve its distribution of connection strengths, degree sequence and density in turn. 
Because such connectivity is non-negative, every one of these networks is dominated by the same mode: the 
whole population rising and falling together. Driven onto it, a network of any size carries essentially a 
single number, leaving nothing to read out. The connectome sits further from that collapse than any null, 
and weight placement is what puts it there. 

We assess computational capacity through two demands that pull on the manifold in opposite ways: retaining past 
inputs and predicting a signal forward when the network runs on its own output. Memory needs many axes carrying 
independent activity, since each holds a different piece of history. They are not the axes variance would identify. 
The manifold's memory lives in hundreds of directions carrying almost none of it. Dimensionality in this setting is
conventionally read from how variance is distributed across the manifold. Measured that way, these networks rank 
almost independently of what they compute, because such measures discount by construction the directions that 
hold the information. We instead count directions by how much each contributes to what can be linearly recovered 
from the state, a ridge-regularised effective rank of the manifold. Against measured memory, this ordering is 
near-perfect where the variance-based one is near-zero.

Prediction makes the opposite demand: not how many axes, but how the trajectory moves along them. 
A path that turns sharply from step to step is one whose next point is hard to extrapolate, and we measure 
this as the mean curvature of the network's trajectory. Being pulled toward the common mode straightens that 
path and helps prediction, while destroying the independent axes memory requires. One geometric property, 
read with opposite sign by the two capacities. 

The connectome's peak memory is at or slightly below that of the nulls. What its weight placement buys is 
not capacity but range: as the gain rises past the point where the nulls have collapsed onto the common mode, 
the connectome is still computing. Connectivity, through the geometry it imposes, sets not how well a network 
computes when the drive is tuned for it, but across how wide a range of drive it computes at all.
