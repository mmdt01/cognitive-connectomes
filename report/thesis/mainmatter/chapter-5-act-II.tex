% P1: what arrives from chapter 4, and the limits that come with it.
Two things arrive from Chapter~\ref{chapter:act-I}, and neither is a statement
about the network's activity. The first is $\mathrm{\textbf{sr\_crit}}$, the spectral radius at which a
substrate's own bulk edge reaches one. Every substrate carries its own value
(Table~\ref{tab:act1-ladder}), which is what makes a second matching axis
available, and also why that axis is not neutral: the substrates differ in one
quantity only, so each axis holds fixed exactly what the other lets vary. The
second is the \textbf{Perron mode}. The previous chapter established that it stands clear of
the bulk and that weight placement is what puts it there. It arrives as
an object to be decomposed rather than as an explanation.

% P2: what the readout sees, the scope, and the question.
What a reservoir's readout sees, however, is not the spectrum of the network's connectivity
but the activity it produces.
Driving a network with an input signal and recording every unit at every timestep
gives an activity state matrix, and everything in this chapter is measured on that matrix. It is the
joint product of two things, the recurrent connectivity that the null ladder varies
and the input signal the network is driven by, and both are held fixed throughout.
Except where Section~\ref{sec:act2-sign} states otherwise, every result below is
measured on the memory-capacity task, in which the input is white
noise, % A2 1
on non-negative weights and at the $N = 448$ parcellation. The question this chapter
asks is which part of the spectrum ends up where in that matrix.


\section{Three captures, and what each can be asked}
\label{sec:act2-captures}
\bigskip

% P1a: what the object is, and why it is the right one to measure.
Everything in this chapter is measured on one object, and it is worth being
concrete about what it is. Drive the reservoir with an input signal and record
every unit at every timestep, and the result is the state matrix
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\mathrm{eff}} \times N}$ of the previous section: a
trajectory through an $N$-dimensional space, one point per timestep, whose shape
is what the phrase \emph{activity manifold} refers to. A linear readout does not
see that trajectory directly. What it sees is the matrix of pairwise inner
products between the units' time series, the \textbf{Gram matrix}
$G = A^{\top}A$, whose entry $(i,j)$ is unit $i$'s time series dotted with unit
$j$'s. That matrix records how the units co-vary and nothing else. It fixes the
shape of the trajectory up to a rotation, discarding the coordinate frame and
keeping the geometry, which is exactly the right thing to keep here: two networks
whose states have the same Gram matrix have the same activity manifold in every
respect a readout could detect, however differently the two are wired. $G$ is also,
literally, the matrix a readout inverts, since fitting a ridge readout means
solving $\hat{w} = (G + \alpha I)^{-1}A^{\top}y$. Its eigenvectors are the
directions the population activity actually explores and its eigenvalues are how
much of the trajectory lies along each of them, so the spectrum of $G$ 
(\emph{not the spectrum of $W$ we have referred to until now}) is two
things at once: a description of the manifold's shape, and the budget a linear
readout has to spend on it. Every measure used below is a function of $G$'s
spectrum.\footnote{$A^{\top}A$ describes the geometry over units. The companion
object $AA^{\top}$ describes the geometry over time, and is where the shape of the
trajectory as a path lives; the two share their non-zero eigenvalues, so
\emph{the Gram spectrum} is unambiguous. This chapter measures the first.
Section~\ref{sec:act2-toact3} returns to why the second is not measured here.}

% P1b: the centring choice, the identity, and the guard.
One choice inside that construction is not a preprocessing detail. As written $G$
is un-centred, so it is dominated by wherever the activity sits on average rather
than by how it moves, and a readout is handed both together. Writing
$m = T_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}A^{\top}\mathbf{1}$ for each unit's mean over time and
$\tilde{A} = A - \mathbf{1}m^{\top}$ for the time-centred states, the two are
related exactly:

\bigskip
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{A^{\top}A \bracegap}_{\text{Gram matrix}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{\tilde{A}^{\top}\tilde{A} \bracegap}_{\substack{\text{fluctuations} \\ \text{about the mean}}}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{T_{\mathrm{eff}}\,m\,m^{\top} \bracegap}_{\substack{\text{rank one:} \\ \text{the time-average}}}
    \label{eq:gram-split}
\end{equation}


% \begin{equation}
%     \underbrace{A^{\top}A \vphantom{\rule[-1.0ex]{0pt}{0pt}}}_{\text{the Gram matrix}}
%     \;=\;
%     \underbrace{\tilde{A}^{\top}\tilde{A} \vphantom{\rule[-1.2ex]{0pt}{0pt}}}_{\substack{\text{fluctuations} \\ \text{about the mean}}}
%     \;+\;
%     \underbrace{T_{\mathrm{eff}}\,m\,m^{\top} \vphantom{\rule[-1.2ex]{0pt}{0pt}}}_{\substack{\text{rank one:} \\ \text{the time-average}}}.
%     \label{eq:gram-split}
% \end{equation}

The second term on the right of Equation \ref{eq:gram-split} is built from one number per unit, 
the level that unit sits at on average. Consequently, the term has \textbf{rank one}: it
describes a single fixed pattern of activity the network holds throughout the run,
and says nothing about how the activity moves. The first term is everything left once
that fixed pattern is subtracted, the part that changes from timestep to timestep
and therefore the only part that can carry information about a changing input.
Centring is the one subtraction that separates them, and it changes what the
leading eigenvector means, which is why this chapter reports both objects and
always says which is in use. The identity settles nothing on its own: it says the
time-average lives in a rank-one term, not what that term consists of, and that is
a measurement, made in Section~\ref{sec:act2-perron}. 

% P1c: what determins the Gram matrix
$G$ is determined jointly by three things: the recurrent matrix, $W$, the operating
point it is scaled to, $\sigma$, and the input driving it, $u(t)$. Tracing how the first of those
shows up in $G$ is what this chapter is for, and the other two are held fixed
throughout so that it can. One thing will not be attempted, however. Nowhere below
is the position of the Gram spectrum obtained analytically from the eigenvalues of
$W$: the nonlinearity and the statistics of the input both intervene, and the link
this chapter draws between the spectral gap and the number of usable directions is
measured, one step at a time, rather than derived. Section~\ref{sec:act2-floor}
supplies a direct reason why no such derivation could work from $W$'s spectrum
alone, since the same recurrent matrix driven at different operating points puts
its Gram spectrum in materially different places.


% P2: the three questions the identity poses.
Equation~\ref{eq:gram-split} poses three questions, and this chapter answers each
from a separate set of measurements, made once and stored, so that nothing below
required a network to be run again. \textbf{How much of the activity sits in the
rank-one term?} That is one number per run: how far the units have all been driven
from zero together. \textbf{What organises the part that is left?} Subtract the
fixed pattern and what remains is a cloud of points in $N$ dimensions, which any
set of $N$ perpendicular axes describes equally well. The informative question is
whether axes read off the network's own structure happen to line up with it, and
two candidates suggest themselves: the eigenvectors of $W$, which are the dynamical
modes themselves, and the low-frequency harmonics of the graph Laplacian, the
smoothest patterns the wiring supports and the network's analogue of low-frequency
Fourier modes~\cite{atasoy2016human}. A third set of axes, drawn at random, sets
the bar that makes the other two readable, since a structural basis that fails to
beat chance has told us nothing. \textbf{And how many directions of that cloud can
the readout use?} That depends on where the eigenvalues of $G$ sit relative to the
regularisation, and it is the question the second half of this chapter turns on.

% P3: the two scope limits that bind later sentences, and the sign fraction.
Two limits on what those measurements can be asked bind everything that follows,
and both are stated here rather than in a limitations section. The first is scope.
The basis comparison was made on two substrates only, the connectome and the
degree-preserving rewire, at two operating points per weight
condition, % COV 3.1, 3.4
so nothing drawn from it can be read as a result about the null ladder, and the
panels of Figure~\ref{fig:F5} each sit at their own condition's operating point
rather than at a shared one. The second is the sign convention. The common-mode
amplitude carries an arbitrary sign, since which way the pattern points is settled
by the input a given run happens to draw, so each run's value is made positive
before runs are combined, never after.\footnote{The order is not a matter of taste.
Combining first can only understate the amplitude, and by an amount that depends on
how many of a substrate's runs straddle zero, which differs between substrates. In
the wrong order the connectome's value at $\sigma = 6$ falls from
0.759 % TIER0 3.7
to 0.638, % TIER0 3.12
and the weight-permuted null falls to 0.575, % A2 5.1
below the connectome, reversing the comparison Section~\ref{sec:act2-perron}
draws.}
One further piece of vocabulary: \emph{supercritical} below means an operating
point of at least 3.05, % TIER0 3.2
the connectome's own critical scale of 3.078 % TIER0 2.1
rounded to the grid, a threshold that is structural rather than chosen to suit the
results and that Section~\ref{sec:act2-counting} shows to be conservative. And
where weight conditions are compared, in Section~\ref{sec:act2-sign}, they are
separate weight families placed side by side. The proportion of negative weights,
$f$, is an intervention applied to the connectome itself in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:act-III}; nothing in this chapter varies it.

% P4: d_eff defined here, justified in section 5.
That leaves the measure. Counting the usable directions of the state matrix needs a
rule, and the rules available by inspection are unsatisfactory: these spectra fall
smoothly across ten orders of magnitude without reaching zero, so every substrate
has full numerical rank, and any cutoff placed by hand would count two otherwise
indistinguishable directions as one and as none. Both approaches treat availability
as a property of the states alone, when whether a direction is available depends on
the readout, which cannot put weight on a direction the regularisation has
suppressed however much structure it holds. So rather than choose a rule, this
thesis takes the one the readout already implies. Ridge regression shrinks each
direction by a factor set by how far its Gram eigenvalue stands above the
regularisation strength $\alpha$, and adding those factors gives the \textbf{\emph{ridge
effective rank}} \footnote{The term \emph{effective rank} is also used for a quantity computed on
the recurrent matrix itself~\cite{clark2025connectivity}. That is a different
object from the one measured here, which is computed on the states the network
produces when driven; the two are related, since the states are the network's, but
they are not the same measurement.},

\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha)
    \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{g_i}{g_i + \alpha}
    \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\!\bigl[G(G + \alpha I)^{-1}\bigr],
    \label{eq:deff}
\end{equation}
where $g_1 \ge \dots \ge g_N$ are the eigenvalues of $G$. Each direction
contributes close to one when its eigenvalue stands well above $\alpha$, close to
zero when it lies well below, and exactly one half at the crossover, so the sum
counts how many directions survive the regularisation with a soft boundary rather
than a sharp one. This is why $\alpha$ is called a \emph{floor} throughout: it is
the level in the spectrum below which a direction stops being worth anything to the
readout. The trace form on the right is what makes this the natural count rather
than a descriptor chosen to suit the argument, since the fitted readout is
$\hat{w} = (G + \alpha I)^{-1}A^{\top}y$ and Equation~\ref{eq:deff} is that
readout's effective degrees of freedom in the standard
sense~\cite{hastie2009elements}. It is a property of the fit, not a measure applied
to it from outside, and $\alpha$ is correspondingly not free: it is the readout's
own value, not a parameter of the measure. Why this count and not a
variance-weighted one is a result rather than a definition;
Section~\ref{sec:act2-counting} makes that case, and making it here would leave the
measure looking assumed.

% P1: what the identity leaves, and the two questions the two panels answer.
Equation~\ref{eq:gram-split} splits the readout's matrix into a rank-one term
carrying the time-average and a fluctuation term carrying everything that moves. It
does not say where the network's own dynamical modes end up between the two, and
that is what this section measures, in two halves. The first asks what organises
the fluctuations once the time-average is gone: if the leading eigenmodes of $W$
are still among them, the decomposition is not clean and the readout is working in
their shadow. 
The second asks how much of the activity the rank-one term consumes,
and whether that depends on the substrate. Figure~\ref{fig:F4} answers them in
turn.

% P3: A2.2 and the inversion.
So the leading modes are absent from the fluctuations. Panel~(b) asks where they
went, tracking the common-mode amplitude across the sweep in $\sigma$ for all four
substrates. Every substrate's amplitude rises as it is driven harder, which is what
a network being pushed into the flat regions of its nonlinearity does, and the
nulls rise first, since they reach their own critical scales well before the
connectome reaches its. What matters is where the curves end up. At
$\sigma = 6$ % TIER0 3.7
the connectome sits at 0.759 % TIER0 3.7
against the nulls' 0.949 to 0.989, % TIER0 3.7
and nearer criticality the gap is wider still. The connectome is the least
common-mode dominated substrate on the ladder, and it is the substrate carrying by
far the largest Perron root. That inversion is the finding of this section, and it
is not a paradox. What distinguishes the connectome is the gap between its leading
eigenvalue and its bulk, rather than a small leading eigenvalue or an unusual bulk,
and a gap of that kind allows the leading mode to be driven hard without the bulk
following it. The nulls, whose bulk sits close behind their Perron root,
synchronise as a whole instead: their units end up pinned near the extremes of the
nonlinearity together, which is what an amplitude approaching one means, and almost
nothing is left over that varies.

% P4: what this establishes and what it does not; poses section 4.
Two things follow, and a third does not. The decomposition is clean, which licenses
the rest of this chapter to work in the fluctuation subspace and treat the
rank-one term as accounted for. And the substrates differ in how much of their
activity that term consumes, in the opposite direction to the one their spectra
naively suggest. What does not follow is anything about capacity. Being least
dominated by the common mode is not the same as having the most directions a
readout can act on, and nothing measured above touches how the fluctuation spectrum
is \emph{arranged}, only how much activity is left in it. The arrangement is what
the readout is finally sensitive to, through the floor of
Equation~\ref{eq:deff}, and it is where the next section goes.

\label{sec:act2-floor}
\bigskip

% P1: the question, the guard, and A2.6 stated as positions.
The previous section showed that the connectome keeps more of its activity in the
fluctuation term than any of its nulls do. It said nothing about how that activity
is arranged, and arrangement is what the readout is sensitive to: what
Equation~\ref{eq:deff} counts is directions standing clear of the floor, so the
question is where each substrate's spectrum sits relative to it. That is measured
here rather than derived, one step at a time, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:act2-captures} gave.

Where that measurement is made is not a neutral choice. Scaling every substrate to
the same nominal radius fixes its leading eigenvalue and leaves the rest of the
spectrum where it falls, so at $\sigma \ge 3.05$ the nulls' bulk sits far further
from the origin than the connectome's. Section~\ref{sec:act2-perron} has already
shown what that costs them: their common-mode amplitude is close to its ceiling
here, and a network whose activity has collapsed into one common pattern has little
fluctuation left for its Gram spectrum to hold up. What follows is therefore a
statement about substrates \emph{at an operating point}, not about substrates, and
the ordering it reports is regional. At low radius it runs the other way, the nulls
clearing more directions than the connectome. % TIER0 3.2

% P2: the rate, with the both-ends warning taken off the algebra first.
Where a spectrum sits and how much it stands to lose are different questions, and
conflating them is easy. Differentiating Equation~\ref{eq:deff} gives the second
one directly:
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\mathrm{d}\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm{d}\log\alpha}
    \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{g_i\,\alpha}{(g_i + \alpha)^{2}} ,
    \label{eq:floor-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
and its shape carries a warning. Each term peaks when a direction sits exactly
\emph{at} the floor and falls away on either side, so the sum is small both for a
spectrum standing well clear of the floor and for one that has already dropped far
below it. A low sensitivity therefore never interprets itself. Erdős–Rényi is the
second case, and reading its low absolute sensitivity as robustness would invert
the truth: per unit of surviving dimensionality it is the \emph{most}
floor-sensitive substrate on the ladder, shedding
29.1\% % TIER0 3.6(c)
of its effective rank per decade of $\alpha$ against the connectome's
5.6\%. % TIER0 3.6(c)
The two lose almost the same number of directions in absolute terms. The connectome
has some four hundred to lose them from, and Erdős–Rényi around seventy-five.

% P3: A2.7 first half, the interior minima.
Neither the position nor the rate is a fixed property of a substrate, because both
depend on where it is being operated. Panel~(b) draws
Equation~\ref{eq:floor-sensitivity} across the sweep, and the curve is two-humped:
it vanishes at zero radius, where a network barely driven has almost no spectrum
above the floor to lose, rises as directions are pushed up through the floor, and
falls again once they stand clear of it. The minima that matter are therefore the
\emph{interior} dips between the two humps, and they fall at different radii for
different substrates: the connectome's near
3.58, % TIER0 3.6(b)
every null's between 1.58 and 2.00, % TIER0 3.6(b)
a separation that holds seed by seed and is not an artefact of taking
medians.\footnote{The qualifier \emph{interior} is not droppable. The connectome's
lowest sensitivity anywhere over non-zero radius lies not at its dip but far out on
the weakly driven limb, % TIER0 3.6(b)
where the reservoir has nothing to lose; read as a global minimum, this half of the
result does not reproduce.}

% P4: A2.7 second half, the migration.
That radius-dependence has a consequence which can be checked. Raising the
regularisation penalises operating points at which many directions sit near the
floor, so each substrate's best operating point should move toward its own interior
dip, and the measured optima do. Across a regularisation grid spanning five orders
of magnitude, % TIER0 3.6(d)
the connectome's ridge-optimal radius moves from
2.4 to 3.6 % TIER0 3.6
and every null moves once, from
1.2 to 1.6, % TIER0 3.6
each toward its own. The agreement is in direction and endpoint only; nothing is
fitted, and the two halves are measured on different radius grids, so the
connectome's dip at 3.58 and its optimum at 3.6 % TIER0 3.6
are two grids' nearest points to one place rather than one number recovered twice.
This is what the comparison at the head of the section amounts to. Each substrate
has an operating point at which its spectrum stands clear of the floor, and the
substrates differ in where that point falls, not in whether they have one. What
distinguishes the connectome is that its own lies higher, so it still sits near it
at radii where the nulls are long past theirs. On the nominal axis that reads as a reversal at low radius, where the nulls clear
more directions than the connectome, giving way to a widening difference above; and
it is a claim about the range of operating points over which a substrate stays
usable rather than about how good any of them is at its best.

% P5: handover into section 5.
One consequence of these positions is worth stating before the next section uses
it. The floor lies some ten orders of magnitude % A2 4.5.3
below the leading variance, so a substrate can have hundreds of directions standing
clear of it which carry almost none of the variance between them. Whether those
directions get counted at all depends on how a dimensionality measure weights them,
and the measure the field reaches for first weights by variance.




\section{Which counting scheme sees it}
\label{sec:act2-counting}

% P1: PR as the default measure, motivated, and the first-person turn.
Measuring the dimensionality of population activity is an active problem in
systems neuroscience, and the standard instrument is the participation ratio.
Given the eigenvalues $\mu_1 \ge \dots \ge \mu_N$ of the time-centred state
covariance, the variance the population puts along each direction, it is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PR}
    \;=\; \frac{\bigl(\sum_i \mu_i\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_i \mu_i^{2}} .
    \label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
It equals the number of directions when variance is spread evenly across them and
falls toward one as a few directions come to dominate, so it answers the question a
neural-manifold analysis usually asks: how many dimensions does the activity
actually occupy? It has good reasons behind it beyond convenience, being closely
related to the quantities that govern how population dimensionality is recovered
from finite recordings~\cite{gao2017theory}, and it is what this work reached for
first.

% P2: the disagreement.
On these substrates it says almost nothing. Across the seven substrates of the
wider null ladder the participation ratio moves
16\%, % TIER0 3.12
from 1.19 to 1.38, % TIER0 3.12
which would ordinarily be read as seven networks whose activity occupies
essentially the same number of dimensions. The ridge effective rank of the same
states moves 5.5-fold, % TIER0 3.12
from 75 to 413 % TIER0 3.12
of the 448 directions available. These are not different experiments. Both
measures are computed from the same recorded states, on the same cells, and one of
them says these substrates are barely distinguishable while the other says they
differ enormously. Measured memory capacity moves roughly
three-fold % A2 2.2
over the same substrates, so there is something there to be seen, and the question
is what makes one measure blind to it.

% P3: both are weighted counts; the mechanism in one cell.
The two are more alike than their disagreement suggests, which is what makes the
disagreement informative. Each is exactly a sum of one weight per direction. For
the ridge effective rank that is Equation~\ref{eq:deff} as written, every direction
weighted by $g_i/(g_i + \alpha)$, how far its Gram eigenvalue clears the
regularisation floor. For the participation ratio it is true but less obvious.
Writing $p_i = \mu_i / \sum_j \mu_j$ for direction $i$'s share of the variance,
Equation~\ref{eq:pr} can be rewritten
\[
    \mathrm{PR} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{p_i}{\sum_j p_j^{2}} ,
\]
so it too is a count, each direction weighted by its share of the variance. Both
are therefore counts, and each curve's area in Figure~\ref{fig:F6}(a) is the number
it is labelled with. What that panel shows, on one connectome cell, is how
differently the two weight the same directions: the ridge weight stands above one
half for 438 of the 448 % A2 2.4
directions, while the variance weight has collapsed by the fifth, with
two directions carrying 95\% % A2 2.4
of a participation ratio of 1.28. % A2 2.4
Both spectra come from one and the same post-warmup state matrix, one before
centring and one after, so the difference between the two counts is a difference of
weighting rather than of which data are used.

% P4: the reason a variance-weighted count misses this is where the spectra sit.
The reason a variance-weighted count misses this is not a defect in the count. It
is where these spectra sit. Section~\ref{sec:act2-floor} measured the floor lying
some ten orders of magnitude below the leading variance, so a direction can stand
far clear of the floor, fully available to the readout, while holding a share of
the variance too small to register in a weighted count. Hundreds of the
connectome's directions are in exactly that position. The participation ratio and
the ridge effective rank have not disagreed about the data. The quantity one
weights by and the quantity that determines availability have come apart, and they
come apart precisely where the previous section found the substrates to differ.

% P5: the test against measured MC, one aggregation unit per sentence.
Whether that matters is a question the data can answer, by asking which measure
tracks what the readout actually achieves. Memory capacity enters here as the
quantity a dimensionality measure is being judged against, not as a performance
claim about any substrate: what is being ordered is the counting schemes, not the
connectome and its nulls. Across the seven substrates, taking one median per
substrate, the ridge effective rank orders them against measured capacity at a rank
correlation of +1.000 % TIER0 3.12
and the participation ratio at +0.107. % TIER0 3.12
Pooled instead over all 350 % TIER0 3.12
individual cells within the supercritical regime, the pair reads
+0.998 % TIER0 3.12
against +0.308. % TIER0 3.12
Those are two separate tests on two aggregation units, and a number from one may
not be set against a number from the other, though the two agree on everything that
matters. Correlating either measure against position in the null ladder is a
different quantity again, weak and negative for both, and is not the claim being
made. One limit belongs with this, since it bounds what the ordering can mean:
every substrate's effective rank peaks within a few per cent of the hard ceiling at
$N$, % TIER0 3.2
so peak dimensionality is unresolvable at this parcellation and the ordering above
lives in the decay region beyond the peak rather than at it.

% P6: observer-relativity owned, then scope.
None of this makes the participation ratio the wrong measure. It measures what it
claims to, how concentrated the variance of the states is, and for the question a
neural-manifold analysis usually asks it remains the right instrument. The claim
here is narrower and, perhaps, more interesting: that quantity is not the one a
ridge readout can act on, so a measure of dimensionality is only meaningful
relative to what is reading the system out. Two measures of the same states can
disagree this sharply without either being in error, and the choice between them is
a question about the observer rather than about the data. 
The demonstration is empirical and its scope should be read narrowly: one task, one
family of substrates, one readout. That total capacity is bounded by the number of
linearly independent state variables is established, and this is a case of that
bound rather than a rival to it~\cite{dambre2012information}. What the case shows
is the link this chapter set out to draw. A large spectral gap leaves the leading
modes carrying the mean rather than the fluctuations; the fluctuations that remain
occupy a Gram spectrum standing well clear of the readout's floor; and the
directions that clear it, hundreds of them, are the ones the readout can use.
Weight placement reaches computational capacity through the geometry of the
activity it produces, and each link in that chain has been measured rather than
assumed.

% P1: the question the decomposition leaves open, and why it is settled here.
One question has been left open since Section~\ref{sec:act2-perron}, and it is
worth returning to now that the previous section has supplied what is needed to
answer it properly. With non-negative weights the leading eigenmodes of $W$ were
absorbed into the rank-one term of Equation~\ref{eq:gram-split}, which leaves the
fluctuations to be organised by something other than the dynamical modes
themselves. What organised them, in Figure~\ref{fig:F4}(a), was the low-frequency
harmonics of the graph Laplacian, the smoothest patterns the wiring supports, which
enter the comparison because connectome-defined harmonic modes have been shown to
structure measured cortical
activity~\cite{atasoy2016human}. That result is easy to read as a fact about the
connectome. It is not. It is a fact about non-negativity, and the way to show as
much is to remove the non-negativity and leave everything else where it is.
Balancing the signs of the weights destroys the Perron guarantee, so there is no
dominant common mode for the leading eigenmodes to be absorbed into, and the
prediction is simply that they should come back.

% P2: A2.3, stated as the swap, with the qualifier that stops the overstatement.
They do. Figure~\ref{fig:F5} repeats the basis comparison across three weight
conditions on one substrate and one task: the empirical weights used throughout
this chapter, the same magnitudes with their signs balanced, and gaussian weights.
At $k = 10$ the harmonics hold 0.168 % TIER0 3.12
of the time-centred variance against the $W$ eigenmodes'
0.004 % TIER0 3.12
with non-negative weights; balancing the signs reverses it to
0.019 against 0.081; % TIER0 3.12
and with gaussian weights the eigenmodes hold
0.522 % TIER0 3.12
against 0.012. % TIER0 3.12
The ordering swaps, it swaps in the same direction on the other two tasks the
capture holds, and the topology never changes: the same graph, the same edges, the
same weight magnitudes in the first two conditions, with only the signs
redistributed. What is claimed is the swap. It is worth being explicit that nothing
stronger is available, because the numbers invite it: on the all-positive substrate
neither basis holds much of anything, the whole range across bases and tasks
running 0.04 to 0.17, % TIER0 3.12
so a claim that the fluctuations live in graph harmonics would overstate what has
been measured. Which basis leads is the measurement; how much either one captures
is a separate question, and the answer to that one is "not much".

The low capture in panel~(a) is not a disappointment but a confirmation, and it is

Section~\ref{sec:act2-counting} found that on this substrate, supercritically, the

\section{The axis that does not move}
\label{sec:act2-temporal}
\bigskip
% Additional section, not in the act2_manifold.md seven-item outline. Carries no
% claim from the register; reports a negative result that bounds the chapter.
% Placed here per CONVENTIONS working rule 8: negative results go where the claim
% they bound is made. No figure: the curvature figure belongs to Act III.
% Sources: fact sheet 12; A2 section 5 item 15; TIER0 3.11.
% FORBIDDEN (sheet 12): bimodality as task-independent; any graded curvature or
% trajectory-straightness account; "the manifold has been fully characterised";
% "curvature is flat, so geometry does not matter"; "sigma_eff crosses 1".

% P1: the second axis, measured and flat.
A trajectory has a shape as well as a spread. Everything above concerns the spread:
how many directions the activity occupies, and how many of them a readout can act
on. The shape is a separate property, 
and the natural way to measure it is the angle between successive steps along
the path, averaged over the trajectory. Because it is computed on displacements
rather than positions, it is unaffected by where the activity sits on average: the
common mode's contribution to the time-average cancels in the difference, so this
is a measurement of shape and not of offset.
A path that runs nearly straight turns by a little; one that reverses
on itself turns by close to $\pi$. 
That quantity was recorded alongside everything else in this chapter, on all three
tasks the captures cover, and it is reported here because it does not move. On the
chaotic prediction task the seed-median curvature is
0.261 % A2 5.15
radians for the connectome and 0.263 % A2 5.15
for Erdős–Rényi, and neither exceeds 0.26 at any operating point in
between; % A2 5.15
over the same range, predictive performance on that task falls by more than
five-fold. % TIER0 3.11
The two noise-driven tasks sit at much higher curvature and are equally flat. That
the levels differ is a fact about the inputs rather than about the networks: a
chaotic attractor is a smooth low-dimensional signal, so successive steps of the
trajectory point in similar directions, whereas a white-noise drive delivers an
independent kick at every timestep and successive displacements are close to
uncorrelated. What the substrate contributes, on all three, is nothing.

% P2: what that does and does not license.
Two readings of this are available and only one is supported. It would be wrong to
conclude that the geometry of the trajectory does not matter, since a quantity that
does not vary cannot explain anything and equally cannot be ruled out. What the
measurement does establish is narrower and more awkward: on the non-negative
weights this chapter is measured on, prediction degrades sharply while the
trajectory's shape stays put, so whatever governs that degradation is not visible
on this axis, and the dimensional account developed above does not reach it either.
The question of what does govern it is left open here and named as open. It is
worth saying plainly that this is a limit on the chapter rather than a detail: the
activity manifold has two axes, and the account offered above is an account of
one.\footnote{One qualifier travels with any later statement about trajectory
shape. Curvature on the chaotic task is sharply bimodal, with under one per cent of
cells falling between the two clusters, but that is a property of the manifold
\emph{and the signal driving it}: on the two noise-driven tasks roughly half of all
cells sit in the band the chaotic task leaves empty. % A2 5.15
The bimodality is real, and it is not general.}



\section{From description to intervention}
\label{sec:act2-toact3}
\bigskip
% Outline item 7 (act2_manifold.md section 4). Carries no new claim and points at
% no figure. Hands over four things, the fourth a boundary rather than a result.
% Sources: fact sheet 12. Does NOT restate the chain, which closes section 5.
% FORBIDDEN here (sheet 12): bimodality stated as task-independent or as a
% property of the manifold; any graded curvature or trajectory-straightness
% account; "the manifold has been fully characterised"; reducing the temporal
% axis to a sentence or an appendix; "curvature is flat, so geometry does not
% matter"; "sigma_eff crosses 1 at the transition"; "the connectome is a better
% reservoir"; "the manifold lives in low-frequency graph harmonics";
% "compact bulk".

% P1: everything so far is correlational. The obvious test, and why it is
% available.
Everything measured in this chapter is a description. The connectome is less
dominated by its common mode than its nulls are, its Gram spectrum stands further
clear of the readout's floor, and it has more usable directions as a result; but
these were observed together, not shown to depend on one another. If the common
mode really is what governs the rest, then removing it should remove the
difference. That test is available, and Section~\ref{sec:act2-sign} has already
shown why: the common mode exists because the weights are non-negative, so
introducing negative weights dissolves it while leaving the topology untouched.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:act-III} therefore grades the proportion of negative weights,
$f$, from zero upward, and asks whether the dimensional advantage disappears along
with the mechanism proposed for it. Where this chapter compared weight families
side by side, the next one intervenes on the connectome itself.

% P2: the radius-dependence is what makes the second reading possible.
The second thing that carries forward is why that test has to be run across
operating points rather than at one. Section~\ref{sec:act2-floor} found that where a
substrate's spectrum sits relative to the floor changes with how hard the network is
driven, so how many directions a readout can use is a property of a substrate at an
operating point rather than of the substrate. That is what allows the same axis to
be read twice in the next chapter: once for how much of a past input a network can
retain, and once for how far into the future it can carry a signal of its own. The
ridge effective rank goes forward as the instrument for the first of those, earned
here rather than assumed, and with its scope attached.

% P3: the boundary, now a back-reference rather than a first report.
The third thing carried forward is the boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:act2-temporal}. Because the temporal axis does not move at
$f = 0$, this chapter reports the spatial one alone; but that axis is not flat
everywhere, and it begins to move once negative weights enter. The intervention
described above therefore runs on both halves of the problem at once, and what it
finds on the second is not a continuation of what this chapter has measured but a
different regime, arriving where the geometry starts to change.
